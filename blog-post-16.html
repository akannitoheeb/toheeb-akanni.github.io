How to Get Your First Freelance Client as a Digital Marketer in Nigeria

TA
Toheeb Akanni July 26, 2026
⏱ 13 min read

When I started freelancing as a digital marketer in Lagos, I had zero clients, zero portfolio, and zero connections in the industry.

I had spent six months learning email marketing, SEO, and content strategy from free YouTube videos and blog posts. I knew the theory. I could talk about funnels, segmentation, and open rates. But I had never been paid to do any of it.

For three weeks, I did what every beginner does. I updated my LinkedIn headline to "Digital Marketing Strategist." I posted motivational quotes about entrepreneurship. I sent cold DMs to business owners offering my services.

The result? One reply. It said: "Do you have any case studies?" I did not. The conversation ended.

Then I changed one thing. Instead of asking people to hire me, I started showing them what I could do — before they ever paid me. I built a simple portfolio page. I documented everything I learned publicly. I offered to fix one specific problem for free, for one hour.

Twenty-one days later, I had my first paying client. A skincare brand in Lekki. ₦50,000 for a one-month email marketing retainer. They are still with me today, and they have referred three other clients.

Here is exactly what I did. No fluff. No "network more" advice. Just the specific steps that work for Nigerian digital marketers starting from zero.

Your first client will not come from your credentials. They will come from your proof. In a market saturated with self-proclaimed experts, the person who demonstrates ability before asking for money wins every time.

— Toheeb Akanni

Why Most Nigerian Digital Marketers Never Get Their First Client

Before I show you what works, let me show you what does not. Because most Nigerian digital marketers are stuck in the same loop I was — and they do not know why.

Here is the typical approach:

  • They lead with their title, not their results. "I am a digital marketer" tells a business owner nothing. Every second person on LinkedIn claims the same title. What have you actually done?
  • They pitch services, not outcomes. "I can manage your social media" is a feature. "I can get you 20 qualified leads per week from Instagram" is an outcome. Business owners buy outcomes.
  • They have no proof. No portfolio. No case studies. No before-and-after screenshots. Just a certificate from an online course and a hopeful attitude. That is not enough in 2026.
  • They target everyone. "I help businesses grow" means you help nobody. The most successful freelancers I know in Nigeria serve one specific niche — real estate, e-commerce, health and wellness, SaaS. Niching down makes you referable.
  • They rely on job boards and Upwork. Competing with freelancers from India and Pakistan on price is a race to the bottom. The best Nigerian clients are not on Upwork. They are on Instagram, LinkedIn, and in WhatsApp business groups.

If this sounds like you, do not worry. I did all of these things too. The fix is simpler than you think.

The 7-Step System to Your First Client

Here is the exact system I used. Each step builds on the last. Skip any step, and the system breaks.

1

Pick One Service and One Niche

You cannot be a social media manager, SEO expert, email marketer, PPC specialist, and content strategist all at once. Not when you are starting. Not when you have no proof.

Pick one service you are genuinely good at. Email marketing. Facebook ads. SEO. Content strategy. Pick the one you would do for free because you enjoy it.

Then pick one niche. Not "small businesses." Not "startups." One specific industry where you understand the customer, the pain points, and the language.

Here are niches that pay well in Nigeria right now:

  • E-commerce (fashion, skincare, supplements)
  • Real estate (agents, developers, short-let managers)
  • Health and wellness (gyms, nutritionists, therapists)
  • Education (tutors, online course creators, schools)
  • B2B services (consultants, agencies, SaaS)

When I started, I chose email marketing for e-commerce skincare brands. Why? I understood the customer journey. I knew the pain points (abandoned carts, low repeat purchases). And I could show results fast.

2

Build a One-Page Portfolio (Even With Zero Clients)

You do not need paid client work to have a portfolio. You need proof of ability. Here is how to create that proof with zero clients:

  • Do a free audit. Pick 5 businesses in your niche. Audit their current marketing. Take screenshots. Write a 1-page report on what they are doing wrong and how to fix it. This is now a portfolio piece.
  • Run a campaign for yourself. Build an email list around a topic in your niche. Grow it to 100 subscribers. Document the open rates, click rates, and growth tactics. This is proof you can do it.
  • Create mock projects. Design a full email sequence for a fictional brand in your niche. Write the copy. Build the automation flow in Brevo. Screenshot everything. This shows process, not just output.
  • Write case-study-style blog posts. Analyze a well-known Nigerian brand's marketing strategy. Break down what they do well and what you would improve. Publish it on LinkedIn or your own blog.

My first portfolio had three items: a free audit I did for a friend's skincare brand, a mock welcome sequence I built in Brevo, and a LinkedIn post analyzing a popular Lagos fashion brand's Instagram strategy. That was enough.

3

Build Your Digital Presence

Before a client hires you, they will Google you. They will check your LinkedIn. They will look for a website. If they find nothing — or worse, an empty LinkedIn profile — you have already lost.

Here is the minimum viable presence:

  • LinkedIn profile optimised for your niche. Headline: "I help [niche] businesses [outcome] through [service]." Not "Digital Marketer | Content Creator | Brand Strategist." One thing. One outcome.
  • One-page portfolio website. Use GitHub Pages (free) + a .name.ng domain (₦500/year). Include: your headline, 3 portfolio pieces, 3 testimonials (even from friends/colleagues if needed), your services, and a contact form.
  • Consistent content. Post one piece of content per week on LinkedIn. A tip. A lesson learned. A breakdown of a campaign you admire. This builds authority over time.

I built my portfolio site at toheebakanni.name.ng for ₦500/year using GitHub Pages. It took one weekend. It has generated more inbound leads than all my cold outreach combined.

4

The "Free Hour" Strategy

This is the single most effective tactic I used to get my first client. And it works because it removes every objection a business owner has.

Here is the pitch:

"I specialise in [service] for [niche] businesses. I would like to offer you a free 1-hour [service] audit. No commitment. At the end, I will send you a short report with 3 things you can implement immediately — whether you hire me or not. If you find it valuable, we can talk about working together. If not, you still keep the report."

Why this works:

  • Zero risk for the business owner. They get value even if they do not hire you.
  • You demonstrate expertise in real time. They see your process, your knowledge, and your professionalism.
  • You uncover real pain points. During the audit, you learn what they actually need — not what you think they need.
  • It creates reciprocity. When you give genuine value first, people feel obligated to return the favour. Many will hire you simply because you helped them.

I sent this pitch to 12 skincare brands on Instagram. 4 replied. 2 booked the free audit. 1 hired me immediately after. The other referred me to a friend two weeks later.

5

Find Clients Where They Already Are

Nigerian business owners are not browsing Upwork at 2 AM. They are on Instagram. They are in WhatsApp business groups. They are at local networking events. Go to them.

Here are the channels that actually work in Nigeria:

  • Instagram DMs (strategic, not spammy). Find businesses in your niche. Engage with their content for 3–5 days first. Like, comment thoughtfully, share their posts. Then send a DM referencing something specific they posted. Lead with value, not a pitch.
  • LinkedIn. Connect with founders and marketing managers in your niche. Comment on their posts with genuine insights. Do not pitch in the first message. Build rapport first. Then offer the free audit.
  • WhatsApp business groups. Join groups where your target clients hang out. Lagos Business Network. E-commerce Nigeria. Real Estate Investors Forum. Participate. Help people. When someone asks a question you can answer, answer it thoroughly. Your expertise will sell itself.
  • Referrals from your network. Tell everyone you know what you do. Your cousin. Your church member. Your former classmates. One referral from a trusted source is worth 100 cold DMs.
  • Local events and meetups. Lagos has regular business meetups, startup events, and industry conferences. Show up. Talk to people. Exchange WhatsApp numbers. Follow up the next day.
6

Price Your First Project to Win, Not to Maximise

Your first client is not about the money. It is about the proof. The testimonial. The case study. The referral.

Here is how to price your first project:

  • Research the market. What do agencies charge for your service? What do mid-level freelancers charge? Price yourself at 30–50% below the mid-level rate for your first project.
  • Offer a clear scope. "I will set up your welcome email sequence in Brevo, write 5 emails, and configure the automation. One-time fee: ₦50,000." Specific scope removes negotiation and scope creep.
  • Include a results clause. "If you do not see at least a 20% increase in welcome email open rates within 30 days, I will revise the sequence for free." This reduces their risk and shows confidence.
  • Ask for a testimonial upfront. "If you are happy with the results, would you be open to a short video testimonial or LinkedIn recommendation?" Most clients will say yes — and that testimonial is worth more than the project fee.

My first project was ₦50,000 for one month. My second was ₦75,000. My fifth was ₦150,000. The price grew as the proof grew. But I would not have gotten to project five without saying yes to project one at a rate that felt low.

7

Over-Deliver, Then Ask for the Next Step

The project is not over when you deliver the work. The project is over when the client is so impressed they cannot stop talking about you.

Here is how to over-deliver:

  • Deliver before the deadline. If you promised Friday, deliver Thursday.
  • Include a bonus they did not pay for. A short Loom video explaining what you built. A one-page guide on how to maintain it. A list of 3 additional improvements they could make next quarter.
  • Follow up after delivery. One week later, check in. "How are the results looking? Any questions?" This shows you care about outcomes, not just invoices.
  • Ask for the next step. "I noticed your abandoned cart emails are not set up yet. That is typically the next highest-ROI project after the welcome sequence. Would you like me to send a proposal?"

My first client started with a ₦50,000 welcome sequence. Two months later, they hired me for a ₦120,000/month retainer covering email automation, segmentation, and monthly campaigns. All because I over-delivered on the first project and followed up consistently.

5 Mistakes That Kill Your First Client

  • Waiting until you feel "ready." You will never feel ready. I did not. My first client hired me before I had ever sent a professional email campaign for a real business. Competence comes from doing, not from waiting.
  • Pitching in the first message. "Hi, I am a digital marketer. Can I manage your ads?" This gets ignored 99% of the time. Build rapport first. Engage with their content. Offer value. Then pitch.
  • Trying to serve everyone. "I help all businesses grow" is the fastest way to help zero businesses grow. Niching down feels limiting, but it is the only way to build a reputation that attracts clients.
  • Underpricing out of desperation. Charging ₦5,000 for a month's work signals that your work has no value. Price low enough to win the first project, but not so low that the client does not respect your time. My floor: never below ₦30,000 for any project, even the first.
  • Ghosting after delivery. Most freelancers deliver the work and disappear. The ones who win are the ones who follow up, check results, and propose the next project. Your first client should become your second, third, and fourth — through upsells and referrals.

Why This Works Specifically in Nigeria

The Nigerian market has unique characteristics that make this system especially effective:

  • Trust is the currency. Nigerian business owners have been burned by freelancers who disappear after payment. When you offer a free audit first, you prove you are not one of them. Trust beats credentials every time.
  • Word-of-mouth is everything. In Nigeria, a referral from a trusted friend is more powerful than any ad. One happy client in Lagos can introduce you to five more. One bad project can close doors forever. Reputation is your real business.
  • Most competitors are lazy. The barrier to entry for digital marketing is low. Anyone can watch a YouTube video and call themselves an expert. But very few will do a free audit, build a portfolio, and follow up consistently. That is your competitive advantage.
  • Payment flexibility matters. Many Nigerian small businesses cannot afford large upfront retainers. Offering a small pilot project (₦30,000–₦50,000) with clear deliverables reduces the barrier to entry. Once they see results, they will pay more.
  • WhatsApp is king. In Nigeria, business happens on WhatsApp. Your portfolio site should have a WhatsApp chat button. Your follow-ups should happen on WhatsApp. Your invoices can even be sent via WhatsApp. Meet clients where they are.

Your First Client Is Closer Than You Think

If you are reading this and you have been trying to get your first client for months, I want you to know: you are probably one decision away.

Not one more course. Not one more certificate. Not one more motivational video.

One decision to stop asking for opportunities and start creating proof.

Build the portfolio. Send the audit offer. Post the content. Have the conversation.

Your first client is not looking for the most experienced marketer. They are looking for someone who understands their problem and can show — not tell — that they can solve it.

Be that person. Start today.
